# Critical Literature Review: Is "Zero RL-for-Dengue-Intervention Papers" a Real Research Gap?

## TL;DR
- **The AEGIS claim is essentially TRUE and defensible:** after extensive searching across arXiv, PubMed/PMC, medRxiv, IEEE, Springer, ScienceDirect and a dedicated verification subagent, I found no published paper that applies a reinforcement-learning agent (Q-learning, DQN, PPO, SAC, actor-critic, RL-solved MDP, or learning bandit) to dengue *intervention sequencing* or vector-control scheduling. Every dengue "control optimization" paper uses classical optimal control (Pontryagin) or is deep-learning *forecasting*.
- **The gap is real, specific, and meaningful but narrow:** RL-for-vector-borne-intervention already exists for malaria (OpenMalaria / KDD Cup 2019 line of work), epidemic RL is a mature post-COVID field (a 2025–26 survey reviewed 19 papers, dominated by COVID-19, with HIV and influenza as the only other named diseases and dengue entirely absent), and dengue is at a record global burden (14,434,584 reported cases in 2024). The gap exists because of disease neglect, community fragmentation, and methodological recency — not because the problem is solved or unimportant.
- **The proposal's specific citations are mostly accurate, with one important correction:** Mai et al. (IJCAI 2023), Bent et al. (AAAI 2018), Miksch et al. (Cebu ABM), and Peng & Perrault (constrained RMAB) check out. However, Corum et al. (arXiv:2601.10967) uses **single/multi-objective mathematical optimization, NOT reinforcement learning** — if AEGIS characterizes it as RL, that is an error.

## Key Findings

**1. Existence check — no true RL-for-dengue-intervention paper exists.** Targeted searches across multiple databases and query formulations consistently returned only two adjacent categories for dengue: (a) classical optimal-control theory papers and (b) ML *forecasting* papers. No learning agent is trained to choose or sequence dengue interventions. The verification subagent, running independent searches, reached the same conclusion with high confidence.

**2. The closest genuine RL-for-vector-control precedent is malaria, never dengue.** Bent et al. (AAAI 2018, "Novel Exploration Techniques (NETs) for Malaria Policy Interventions") frame malaria intervention selection over OpenMalaria as a stochastic multi-armed bandit and compare with policy gradients. This was extended by the IBM Research Africa/Hexagon-ML KDD Cup 2019 "Humanity RL" challenge — e.g., Nguyen, Belaid et al., "Policy Learning for Malaria Control" (arXiv:1910.08926), in which "Q-Learning with sequence breaking has been submitted to the challenge and got ranked 7th in KDD Cup," compared against Genetic Algorithm, Bayesian Optimization and Random Search over a 5-year ITN/IRS intervention horizon — and by follow-ups including "An Analysis of Reinforcement Learning for Malaria Control" (arXiv:2107.08988) and "Data-Efficient RL for Malaria Control" (arXiv:2105.01620, using VB-MCTS). That malaria has this body of work while dengue has none makes the gap specific and credible.

**3. Epidemic RL is a mature, COVID-dominated field that simply has not reached dengue.** The post-COVID explosion of epidemic-RL work is real: Khadilkar et al. (lockdown RL, 2020), Libin et al. (deep RL large-scale epidemic control, 2020), Kompella et al., Colas et al., and the EpiPolicy line. A recent survey (Liu, Liu & Liu, "Empowering Epidemic Response," arXiv:2603.25771) screened 49 PubMed publications (2020–July 2025) and reviewed 19, organized into resource allocation, lives-vs-livelihoods, mixed multi-intervention policy, and inter-regional coordination. The reviewed literature is overwhelmingly COVID-19, with HIV (one Q-learning paper) and influenza as the only other named diseases. Dengue and vector-borne diseases are entirely absent from this survey's taxonomy.

**4. Extensive classical optimal-control work on dengue exists — this is the true "near-miss" body.** There is a deep literature solving dengue intervention problems with Pontryagin's Maximum Principle over SEIR-SEI / SIR-SI host-vector models (e.g., Rodrigues, Monteiro & Torres; Bulletin of Mathematical Biology 2020; European Physical Journal Plus 2020/2021; AIMS Math 2023/2025; a Western Sydney University SEIR-SEI four-control framework covering awareness, clinical management, adulticide, larvicide). These derive open-loop optimal schedules from known ODE parameters — they do not *learn* a closed-loop policy from data or simulation interaction, which is exactly the RL distinction AEGIS would exploit.

**5. Dengue ML is overwhelmingly applied to forecasting, not intervention.** LSTM, random forest, LASSO, CNN-RNN hybrids, and ensembles dominate dengue ML (Brazil, Malaysia, Colombia, Philippines), predicting incidence weeks ahead. A 2025 technical narrative review notes AI is reshaping dengue surveillance and forecasting but that vector control "is often reactive, based on outdated entomological indicators." The decision layer — when/where/how to intervene — remains un-optimized by learning methods.

## Details

### Why the gap exists (hypothesis-by-hypothesis assessment)

**(a) Data availability — STRONG contributor.** Dengue surveillance is sparse, noisy, and under-reported. WHO states many endemic countries lack strong detection/reporting and that the true global burden is underestimated; a large share of infections are asymptomatic — a pooled prevalence of 59.26% asymptomatic (95% CI: 43.76–74.75), per Asish et al., Int J Infect Dis 2023 (ICMR-National Institute of Epidemiology) systematic review/meta-analysis of 41 studies (131,953 cases), with 65.52% during outbreaks vs 30.78% in non-outbreak periods. This makes model-free RL directly on real data nearly infeasible — but it does not block simulator-based RL (which is how malaria RL is done).

**(b) Simulator fidelity — MODERATE contributor.** Validated dengue simulators exist (Miksch et al.'s Cebu City ABM calibrated to the 2010 epidemic; the PLOS ONE ABM decision-analysis paper) but are heavier and less standardized than OpenMalaria, which had a ready Gym-style environment that catalyzed malaria RL. There is no widely adopted, RL-ready dengue environment — a key structural reason malaria got RL first.

**(c) Problem significance / funding — STRONG underlying driver.** Dengue is a WHO-classified neglected tropical disease. HIV/AIDS, TB and malaria have historically captured over 70% of all neglected-disease R&D funding — per the Policy Cures Research G-FINDER survey (WHO Global Observatory, 2022 data): "investments in R&D for these three diseases account for over 70% of total investments in neglected diseases with US$ 1,352 million for HIV/AIDS, US$ 702 million for tuberculosis, and US$ 604 million for malaria" (the G-FINDER 2024 report puts the big-three at ~80% of disease-specific funding in 2023). Dengue sits in a second tier receiving low single-digit percentages. Yet burden is now enormous and rising: 2024 was the worst year on record — per WHO's "Dengue: global situation, surveillance and progress – 2024 update," "In 2024, dengue was transmitted at unprecedented levels worldwide. WHO received reports of 14434584 cases" (including 7,718,585 lab-confirmed, 52,738 severe and 11,201 deaths across all six regions), with the Region of the Americas accounting for >90% of the total and Brazil alone reporting >10 million cases. The mismatch between burden and research infrastructure is precisely the structural neglect that produces gaps like this one.

**(d) Methodological maturity — STRONG, and the most charitable explanation.** Epidemic RL only matured post-2020. Even the dedicated survey's earliest in-scope works are 2020. The field has worked outward from COVID; it simply has not yet reached vector-borne diseases beyond malaria. The gap is plausibly a matter of timing/diffusion, not impossibility.

**(e) Domain fragmentation — STRONG contributor.** RL epidemic work publishes at AI venues (AAAI, IJCAI, NeurIPS, AAMAS); dengue control work publishes in tropical-medicine, math-biology, and entomology venues. The two communities rarely interface, and malaria RL only happened because an industrial AI lab (IBM Research Africa) engaged a malaria simulator. No comparable bridge has formed for dengue.

**(f) Sufficient existing solutions — WEAK; the gap is NOT because current practice works well.** Practitioners describe current practice as inadequate. The Philippine DOH uses a fixed "4S/5S" strategy (Search and destroy, Seek early consultation, Self-protection, Say yes to fogging only during outbreaks) deployed mainly at the onset of the rainy season; researchers argue this should be year-round as warming reduces seasonality. The Lancet editorial "Dengue: the threat to health now and in the future" (2024) states verbatim: "The seasonality of dengue and the periodic nature of outbreaks means that control measures are often implemented reactively, during the peak in cases—too little, too late." This is an argument *for* optimization, strengthening AEGIS's motivation.

**(g) Vector-borne (SEIR-SEI) complexity — GENUINE but surmountable technical factor.** The two-population host-vector coupling roughly doubles the state space versus SEIR, introduces fast (mosquito) and slow (human) timescales, and adds vector-specific controls (adulticide, larvicide, Wolbachia) with delayed effects — making credit assignment and reward shaping harder. This is real added difficulty but not prohibitive; classical optimal control already handles SEIR-SEI routinely.

**(h) Geography / resource — STRONG underlying driver.** Dengue is concentrated in lower- and middle-income tropical countries with less ML research infrastructure, reinforcing both the funding and fragmentation drivers.

**Synthesis of "why":** The gap is real and best explained by the *intersection* of (c) neglect/funding, (d) methodological recency, (e) community fragmentation, and (h) geography, with (a) data sparsity and (b) the absence of an RL-ready simulator as the concrete technical blockers. (f) — "existing solutions are good enough" — is *not* a valid explanation; practitioners actively describe current decision-making as reactive and suboptimal.

### How big is the gap?
- Epidemic RL papers number in the dozens-to-low-hundreds depending on scope; the focused survey reviewed 19 core intervention papers, dominated by COVID-19.
- Dengue research output annually is large and ML *is* being applied — but almost entirely to forecasting/diagnosis, not intervention policy.
- Intervention decision-making (timing/targeting of vector control) is explicitly flagged as an unsolved, reactive problem by WHO, the Lancet, and Philippine operational literature.
- Net: the *intersection* (dengue ∩ RL ∩ intervention sequencing) is genuinely empty, while each individual circle is well-populated. That is the hallmark of a legitimate, fillable research gap.

### Quality-control check on AEGIS's cited papers
- **Mai et al., IJCAI 2023 (arXiv:2301.12802) — ACCURATE.** "Planning Multiple Epidemic Interventions with Reinforcement Learning" applies PPO and SAC over EpiPolicy ODE-based models, representing multiple continuous interventions, and outperforms hand-crafted baselines. If AEGIS says it "uses PPO over EpiPolicy," that is correct (it uses PPO *and* SAC; PPO outperforms SAC).
- **Peng & Perrault, arXiv:2603.19397 — ACCURATE.** "Optimizing Resource-Constrained Non-Pharmaceutical Interventions for Multi-Cluster Outbreak Control Using Hierarchical RL" formulates the problem as a constrained restless multi-armed bandit solved with hierarchical RL. Matches the description.
- **Miksch et al. 2016 — ACCURATE.** Agent-based dengue model calibrated to the 2010 Cebu City epidemic; later extended in a 2019 PLOS ONE decision-analysis paper. A simulation/ABM study, not RL — consistent with using it as a simulator precedent.
- **Corum et al., arXiv:2601.10967 — MISCHARACTERIZATION RISK.** "Balancing Economic Cost and Disease Impact: Optimization Models for Wolbachia-Based Dengue Control" (Philippine context, ~PHP 17 billion annual dengue cost) uses single- and multi-objective mathematical optimization, NOT reinforcement learning. If AEGIS cites it as an RL paper, correct this; cite it as a classical-optimization near-miss.
- **Bent et al., AAAI 2018 — ACCURATE.** "Novel Exploration Techniques (NETs) for Malaria Policy Interventions" frames malaria policy as a stochastic multi-armed bandit over OpenMalaria, with comparison to policy gradients. Correctly characterized as malaria RL.

## Recommendations
1. **Keep the central claim but tighten the wording.** State it as: "To our knowledge, no published work applies reinforcement learning to *dengue intervention sequencing*; existing dengue control-optimization work uses classical optimal control, and existing dengue ML targets forecasting." This is fully defensible and immune to a single-counterexample rebuttal. Avoid the unqualified "zero papers" phrasing in favor of "to our knowledge, after systematic search of arXiv/PubMed/IEEE/Scopus."
2. **Fix the Corum et al. characterization.** Reclassify it as mathematical (multi-objective) optimization, not RL. Use it as evidence that even Philippine dengue intervention work stops short of RL.
3. **Frame the gap with the malaria analogy.** The strongest motivating argument is "RL has been applied to malaria vector-control policy since 2018 but never to dengue" — concrete, specific, and hard to dispute.
4. **Pre-empt the "why hasn't this been done" reviewer question** by naming the real blockers: absence of an RL-ready, validated dengue simulator (vs. OpenMalaria), surveillance data sparsity, NTD funding neglect, and AI/epidemiology community fragmentation. Consider committing to releasing a Gym-style dengue SEIR-SEI environment as a contribution — this directly addresses blocker (b).
5. **Cite current-practice inadequacy** (DOH 4S/5S fixed seasonal schedule; the Lancet's "too little, too late") as the applied motivation, and the record 2024 burden (14,434,584 cases) as the urgency hook.
6. **Benchmarks that would change the assessment:** if a reviewer surfaces a paper that (i) trains a learning agent (ii) on a dengue/Aedes transmission model (iii) to choose or time interventions, the novelty claim weakens from "first" to "among the first." Monitor arXiv q-bio.PE, cs.LG, and eess.SY for late-2025/2026 preprints, since the field is diffusing toward vector-borne diseases.

## Caveats
- **Negative results cannot be proven absolutely.** Database search (even with a dedicated subagent) cannot rule out an obscure conference paper, thesis, or non-English preprint using non-standard terminology. The claim should always be hedged as "to our knowledge after systematic search."
- **Terminology boundary issues.** Some bandit-based malaria/public-health RMAB work and "RL recommendation for vector-borne disease" classification papers (e.g., RNN-based "reinforcement learning" recommendation for VBD diagnosis) use RL-adjacent language but are not intervention-sequencing; a careless reviewer could miscite these. Define your scope precisely (sequential intervention policy over a transmission model).
- **The survey arXiv:2603.25771 dating is unusual** (ID and stated date imply 2026); it is real and accessible but treat its vintage as 2025–2026. Its support for the dengue gap is by *omission* (dengue appears nowhere in its 19 papers or references), not an explicit "dengue has not been studied" statement — cite it accordingly.
- **Funding figures vary by year and source** (G-FINDER); the "big three capture >70% of neglected-disease R&D" figure is directional (2022 data; ~80% in the 2023 G-FINDER report), not a fixed constant.
